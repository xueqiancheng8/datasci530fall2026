Who Moves the Bills? Text Analysis of Congressional Bill Actions¶

DATASCI 530 - Assignment 4
Author: Xueqian Cheng

Summary of findings¶

I used regular expressions to pull names, procedures and purposes out of the free-text descriptions of 3,303 actions on legislation in the 116th U.S. Congress (2019-2021).

  • Data. The actions cover 1,494 distinct bills (bill type + number). Amendments make up 46% of rows, and House floor actions (31%) are the most common type of action.
  • Senate bills in the House. There are 116 House floor actions on Senate bills. 68% are motions to suspend the rules, a fast-track procedure that needs a two-thirds vote. 16% are suspensions as amended, where the House changed the Senate text.
  • Who moves them. 53 House members appear. The median member moved a single Senate bill. A few committee chairs moved many, led by Takano (Veterans' Affairs) with 9.
  • Senators on floor amendments. Among 286 Senate floor amendments, 69% name two senators: one offers the amendment for another. No amendment names more than two. Majority Leader McConnell offered amendments for 57 different senators and Reed for 25, so these pairs reflect floor procedure more than coauthorship.
  • New insight. Classifying each amendment's stated purpose shows that substantive amendments (substitutes, "to require", "to provide") are usually offered by proxy. Placeholder amendments ("to improve the bill", "to change the enactment date") are offered by McConnell himself, which shows the Majority Leader "filling the amendment tree".

2.1 Suspensions of the rules¶

"Suspending the rules" is a fast-track procedure in the House: debate is limited to 40 minutes, no amendments can be offered from the floor, and the bill needs a two-thirds majority.

Coding decisions.

  • An action counts as a suspension if its text contains the motion moved to suspend the rules. I do not simply search for the word "suspend": one "ORDER OF BUSINESS" entry only mentions a future motion to suspend (extending its debate time) and is not itself a motion.
  • An action counts as suspended as amended if the motion ends with , as amended, meaning the House changed the Senate text before passing it. These are a subset of the suspensions.

2.2 Who moves Senate bills on the House floor?¶

Every House floor action starts with the member who made the motion, e.g. "Ms. Kelly (IL) asked unanimous consent ..." or "Ms. Horn, Kendra S. moved to suspend ...".

Regular expression. (Mr|Ms|Mrs)\. (.+?)(?= moved| asked)

  • (Mr|Ms|Mrs)\. - the title, captured separately;
  • (.+?) - the name: the shortest run of characters...
  • (?= moved| asked) - ...up to the verb (a look-ahead, so the verb itself is not captured).

Stopping at the verb, instead of taking a single word, keeps multi-word names intact ("Jackson Lee", "Torres Small (NM)", "Horn, Kendra S."). The state in brackets is kept because the House uses it to tell apart members with the same surname (e.g. Larsen (WA) vs. Larson (CT)).

3. Senate floor amendments: how many senators per amendment?¶

The subset here is main_action == "senate amendment proposed (on the floor)". A typical text is

S.Amdt.2341 Amendment SA 2341 proposed by Senator Reed for Senator Wyden to Amendment SA 2301 ...

When two senators appear, the first (Senator 1) is the senator who formally offered the amendment on the floor, and the second (Senator 2) is the senator on whose behalf it was offered, usually the real author.

Unit of observation. Each row is one amendment attached to a bill, and a bill can receive many amendments (one bill in this subset has 52). Counting senators "per bill" therefore means counting them per bill action (per amendment row); combining all amendments of a bill would make "Senator 1" and "Senator 2" meaningless.

Regular expression. Senator ([A-Z][a-zA-Z]+(?: (?:[A-Z][a-z]+|[A-Z]{2}))?)

  • Senator - the literal title;
  • [A-Z][a-zA-Z]+ - a capitalized surname (allows internal capitals, as in McConnell, McSally);
  • (?: ...)? - optionally a second capitalized word, either another part of the surname (Van Hollen, Cortez Masto) or a two-letter state that tells apart same-name senators (Scott FL vs. Scott SC).

4. Summary of insights¶

  • The House is busier on paper. House bills and House floor/amendment actions make up most of the dataset; Senate floor amendments are under 9% of actions.
  • Senate bills move through the House on the fast track. Two-thirds of House floor actions on Senate bills are motions to suspend the rules and almost all of the rest are unanimous-consent requests. Only 18 of 116 actions change the Senate text ("as amended").
  • A few committee chairs carry most Senate bills in the House. The number of Senate bills per member is highly skewed, with chairs such as Takano and Pallone at the top.
  • Senate floor amendments are usually offered by proxy. About two-thirds name two senators, and leaders (McConnell, Reed) account for most of the "partner" links. The pairs reflect floor procedure more than personal collaboration.

5. New insight: what do Senate floor amendments try to do?¶

(f) Besides who offers them, the text of each Senate amendment ends with a short statement of its purpose, for example "To require the Secretary of the Army to submit ..." or "In the nature of a substitute." I extract that statement with a regular expression and classify it by its first verb.

Regular expressions.

  1. \)\s*(.+)$ - the purpose is everything after the closing bracket of the Congressional Record reference (consideration: ... ). Two amendments have no such reference; for them the fallback Senator [A-Z][\w ]*?\.\s*(.+)$ takes the text after the senator's name.
  2. ^To (\w+) - for purposes starting with "To", the next word is the verb (require, provide, ...). Exception: the boilerplate texts "To improve the bill." and "To change the enactment date." say nothing about content. They are grouped as placeholder amendments. Purposes that do not start with "To" are procedural ("In the nature of a substitute", "Of a perfecting nature") and are labelled by their own text.

senate_amendments["purpose"] = (senate_amendments["action"].str.extract(r"\)\s*(.+)$")[0]
                                 .fillna(senate_amendments["action"].str.extract(r"Senator [A-Z][\w ]*?\.\s*(.+)$")[0])

print(f"Amendments without an extracted purpose: {senate_amendments['purpose'].isna().sum()}")

def classify_purpose(text):
    '''Return the verb after "To", or a procedural label.'''
    if pd.isna(text):
        return "No purpose stated"
    if text.startswith("To improve the bill") or text.startswith("To change the enactment date"):
        return "Placeholder (\"To improve the bill\", etc.)"
    verb = re.match(r"^To (\w+)", text)
    if verb:
        return "To " + verb.group(1).lower()
    if re.search(r"substitute", text, flags=re.IGNORECASE):
        return "Substitute (replace full text)"
    if re.search(r"perfecting", text, flags=re.IGNORECASE):
        return "Perfecting (technical)"
    return "Other"

senate_amendments["purpose_type"] = senate_amendments["purpose"].apply(classify_purpose)
senate_amendments["n_senators"] = amendment_senators["n_senators"]

(g) What do we learn?

  • Most floor amendments make real policy changes. The largest group is amendments "in the nature of a substitute", which replace the whole text of a bill, typically with a negotiated version. Next come amendments that require an agency to act, amend existing law, or provide funds.
  • Substantive amendments travel by proxy. Over 80% of "To require", "To amend" and "To provide" amendments, and about 80% of substitutes, are offered by one senator on behalf of another. This matches the leaders' role found in Section 3: individual senators write the content and leaders bring it to the floor.
  • Procedural amendments are offered by the leader himself. The placeholder amendments ("To improve the bill", "To change the enactment date") and the "perfecting" amendments are mostly offered by McConnell alone, with no second senator: 29 of these 37 amendments. This pattern is known as "filling the amendment tree": the Majority Leader takes up every available amendment slot with placeholder text so that no other senator can offer amendments.
  • Together with Section 3, this shows that the same "Senator 1" can play two very different roles: a broker for colleagues' substantive amendments, and a gatekeeper who uses placeholder amendments to control the floor.
